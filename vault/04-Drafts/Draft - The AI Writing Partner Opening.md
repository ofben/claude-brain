# The AI Writing Partner — Opening Options

Trying to nail the opening for this piece. Sarah said "show us your process" so I need something personal but not navel-gazing. Three attempts below. Need to pick one (or frankenstein them together) before I can move forward with the rest of the draft.

---

## Opening A — The Scene

It's 11 PM on a Tuesday and I'm staring at a cursor blinking on an empty page. The Horizon piece is due in two weeks. I have six pages of research notes, three half-formed angles, and the growing suspicion that I've been overthinking this for days. I've written professionally for six years. The blank page should not still intimidate me. And yet.

I open a new tab and type a prompt into Claude: *"I'm writing a feature about how writers use AI tools. I have too many angles and can't find the thread. Can you help me think through this?"*

What comes back isn't my article. It isn't even close. But it's a mirror held up to my own scattered thinking — organized, distilled, and slightly too clean. And in the gap between what the AI suggests and what I actually want to say, I find my angle. Not the AI's angle. Mine. The tool didn't write for me. It helped me think.

That moment — the one where a machine helps a human find their own voice — is the story I want to tell. Because it's happening in writing rooms and newsrooms and coffee shops all over the world right now, and we don't have a good vocabulary for it yet.

Risk: readers who are anti-AI might check out before I can show the nuance. Maybe soften "11 PM on a Tuesday" — too confessional?]*

## Opening B — The Question

Here's a question I've been arguing with myself about for the better part of a year: does AI make me a better writer, or is it making me a lazier one?

I don't mean this theoretically. I mean it personally, specifically, uncomfortably. I'm a freelance writer. I use AI tools — Claude, ChatGPT, occasionally others — in my work. Not to write my articles (I'm writing this one right now, word by word, no autocomplete in sight). But to brainstorm when I'm stuck. To summarize research I don't have time to re-read. To argue against my own thesis and find the holes. To draft the emails I don't want to write so I can save my creative energy for the writing I do.

Is that cheating? Is it efficiency? Is it the same thing every writer has always done — reaching for whatever tool helps them think — just with a new kind of tool?

I don't have a clean answer. But I've spent the last three months talking to writers who are wrestling with the same question, and what I found is more complicated, more human, and more interesting than the headlines suggest.

*[NOTE TO SELF: Stronger thesis but might alienate people upfront. The "does it make me lazier" framing is honest and I think readers will relate, but it sets up a binary that I then have to dismantle. Also — starting with "here's a question" is a structure I've used before. Is it getting stale? The strength is that it immediately establishes the personal stakes. Sarah might like that this one has more intellectual energy.]*

## Opening C — The Data

Seventy-three percent of professional writers have now tried an AI writing tool at least once. But only 28 percent use one regularly. That gap — between curiosity and commitment, between trying and trusting — tells you almost everything you need to know about where we are in the AI-and-writing story.

The AuthorGuild survey from January 2026 that produced those numbers also found something the headlines mostly missed: the most common use of AI among writers isn't drafting. It's brainstorming. Writers aren't asking AI to write for them. They're asking it to think with them — to generate possibilities they can react to, push back against, and ultimately discard on their way to their own ideas.

This is not the story of robots replacing writers. It's the story of writers quietly, awkwardly, and sometimes guiltily figuring out how to use a strange new tool without losing the thing that makes their work theirs. It's messier than the tech evangelists want it to be and less dire than the doomsayers predict. And it's happening right now, in real time, without a playbook.
