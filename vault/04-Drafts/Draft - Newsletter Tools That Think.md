# Tools That Think With You

*Draft for The Synthesis, Issue #33*

---

I have a confession: my notes are a disaster.

Not metaphorically — literally. Right now, as I write this, I have research for three different articles scattered across Google Docs. I have newsletter ideas in Apple Notes, client briefs buried in email threads, a text file on my desktop called "ideas.txt" that I'm afraid to open, and about forty browser tabs I've been meaning to "get to" since January.

Sound familiar?

I've been a professional writer for six years. I read constantly, research obsessively, and think in connections — linking one idea to another is basically my job description. And yet I have never had a system for capturing and connecting those ideas. My brilliant insight from last Tuesday's research session? Gone. That perfect analogy I thought of on a walk? Lost to the void. The thread between two articles I'm working on for different clients? I can feel it exists, but I can't find the notes to prove it.

Last week, I fell down a rabbit hole that might change this. And because I believe in sharing the journey — not just the polished result — I want to tell you about it in real time.

---

## The Tool Paradox

Before I tell you what I found, let me tell you what I've already tried.

Over the past six years I've used: Google Docs, Notion, Evernote, Apple Notes, Bear, a physical Moleskine (three of them, actually, all half-filled), loose-leaf paper, voice memos, email drafts as a notes app (don't judge me), and, for one ambitious week in 2024, a custom Airtable setup that I never looked at again.

Each time I adopted a new tool, there was a brief honeymoon period. I'd migrate my notes, set up my folders, feel the rush of a clean system. And then, gradually, the system would decay. Notes would pile up unsorted. I'd start capturing ideas somewhere else "just for now." Within a month, I'd be back to chaos with one more abandoned app on my phone.

Here's the paradox I kept running into: **more tools didn't mean more organized thinking. They meant more places for ideas to hide.**

The problem was never the tool. The problem was that I was treating note-taking as storage — putting information somewhere so I could find it later. And storage is fine for files and photos. But ideas aren't files. Ideas are alive. They connect to other ideas. They evolve. They matter most when they bump into something unexpected and create something new.

I was filing my ideas away like I was putting them in a drawer. And then I was surprised when they never came back out.

## What Makes a Tool Think With You

So if storage isn't the answer, what is?

I've been reading about this — a lot — and I think the tools that genuinely augment thinking share a few key properties. Here's what I've found so far.

**They connect, not just collect.** The difference between a note-taking app and a thinking tool is links. Not folder hierarchies, not tags (though tags help), but direct connections between ideas. When I write a note about [[AI Tools Landscape 2026|AI tools for writers]], I should be able to link it to my note about [[Tools for Thought History|the history of tools for thought]] and see that connection reflected in both places. The link IS the thinking.

**They're low friction.** If it takes more than 30 seconds to capture a thought, the thought will evaporate. The tool needs to get out of the way. Markdown is brilliant for this — no formatting menus, no templates you have to fill out, just text and structure.

**They reward revisiting.** Most of my notes are write-once-read-never. A good thinking tool makes old notes surface naturally — through backlinks, through search, through the organic process of linking new ideas to existing ones. The system should surprise you by showing you what you already know.

**They're yours.** Not locked in someone's cloud, not dependent on a subscription, not formatted in a proprietary way that traps your thinking inside a product. Local files. Plain text. Portable.

## The AI Layer

- The most interesting frontier: what happens when you combine networked notes with AI?
- Not AI writing your notes for you — AI helping you SEE patterns across your notes
- The dream tool: something that sits on top of your knowledge base and says "hey, your research for Client A connects to this idea you had for Client B"
- This doesn't fully exist yet but it's close — Notion AI, Mem, some Obsidian plugins are moving in this direction
- The [[AI Tools Landscape 2026]] is evolving so fast that by the time this newsletter goes out, something new will have launched
- Connection to the Horizon piece — AI as augmentation, not replacement (Engelbart's vision from 1968!)
- The question: do we want AI organizing our thoughts? Or do we want to do the connecting ourselves, with AI as a safety net for what we miss?
