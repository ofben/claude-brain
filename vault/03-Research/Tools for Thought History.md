# Tools for Thought — A History

Went down a serious rabbit hole on this. The idea that we can build tools to augment human thinking isn't new — it goes back 80 years. Documenting what I found because it's relevant to the [[AI Writing Partner - Brief|Horizon piece]], the [[Issue Draft - Tools That Think With You|newsletter draft]], and honestly my own understanding of what I'm trying to do with this vault.

## The Lineage

### Vannevar Bush — "As We May Think" (1945)
Bush was a science advisor to FDR during WWII. After the war, he wrote this essay in The Atlantic arguing that scientists were drowning in information and needed better tools to manage it. He imagined a device called the **Memex** — a desk with microfilm readers where you could store all your books, records, and communications, and create "trails" of links between related documents.

The Memex was never built but the concept is basically... the web. Hyperlinks. Personal knowledge management. Bush described it 50 years before it existed. He even talked about the problem of information overload — in 1945! If it was a problem then, no wonder we're drowning now.

Key quote: "The human mind operates by association. With one item in its grasp, it snaps instantly to the next that is suggested by the association of thoughts."

This is exactly the principle behind the [[Zettelkasten Method]] and tools like Obsidian. Your notes should work the way your mind works — by association, not by hierarchy.

### Doug Engelbart — "The Mother of All Demos" (1968)
Engelbart demonstrated the mouse, hypertext, video conferencing, and collaborative real-time editing — IN 1968. Before the personal computer existed. He was working on what he called "augmenting human intellect" — using computers not to replace human thinking but to make it more powerful.

His NLS (oN-Line System) was the first real attempt at building what we'd now call a knowledge work environment. It had outlining, cross-referencing, version control — concepts that took decades to become mainstream. The demo was so far ahead of its time that people in the audience literally didn't know what they were watching.

His framework: the tool should be a bicycle for the mind (a phrase Jobs later popularized). You can walk on your own, but a bicycle amplifies your effort and speed. Same idea with thinking tools.

What strikes me: Engelbart wasn't interested in automation. He wanted *augmentation*. There's a crucial difference, and it's exactly the distinction I'm trying to make in the Horizon piece about AI writing tools.

### Ted Nelson — Hypertext & Project Xanadu (1960s-ongoing)
Nelson coined the term "hypertext" and spent decades trying to build Project Xanadu, his vision of a universal hypertext system. He imagined a world where all text was interconnected, where you could see the history of every document, where links went both ways.

The web we got (Tim Berners-Lee's version) is a simplified version of Nelson's vision. Nelson is still kind of bitter about it. His ideas were more ambitious — bidirectional links, visible connections, transclusion (embedding one document inside another while maintaining the link to the original).

Obsidian's backlinks feature is actually closer to Nelson's vision than regular web links are. When I link to a note, that note KNOWS it's been linked to. That's bidirectional linking. That's what Nelson wanted.

### Howard Rheingold — "Tools for Thought" (1985/2000)
Rheingold wrote the book that gave the movement its name. He traced the history from Bush through Engelbart to personal computing. His argument: these tools aren't just about productivity. They're about extending what humans are capable of thinking.

Haven't read the whole thing (it's dated) but his central thesis holds up.

### Modern Era
The "tools for thought" concept has been revived by a new generation of tool builders:
- **Roam Research** (2020) — Conor White-Sullivan built a tool that brought bidirectional linking and block-level references to mainstream note-taking. Sparked the whole "networked thought" movement. It was genuinely revelatory for a lot of people, even if the tool itself has been overtaken by competitors.
- **Obsidian** (2020) — Local-first, markdown files, powerful linking and graph views. Basically a modern Zettelkasten. The community plugins are incredible — you can make it do almost anything. This is what I'm using now and it feels right.
- **Notion** — More structured, database-oriented. Great for teams and project management. Less "tools for thought" and more "tools for organizing," but the AI features are pushing it toward the thinking-tool space.
- **Andy Matuschak** — Former Apple/Khan Academy, now independent researcher. His public working notes are a living example of networked thinking. His concept of "evergreen notes" is basically Zettelkasten principles with better branding.
- **Gordon Brander** — Building Subconscious, exploring "tools for thought" at a protocol level

## The Common Thread
Every person in this lineage is trying to solve the same problem: **human thinking is limited by our working memory and our ability to see connections across large bodies of knowledge.** The right tools can help us overcome those limits.

This isn't about replacing thinking. It's about building infrastructure for better thinking.

## Why This Matters Now
We're in a moment where:
1. Information overload is worse than ever (Bush's 1945 problem, multiplied)
2. AI tools can help process and connect information (Engelbart's augmentation dream?)
3. Tools like Obsidian make networked note-taking accessible (not just for academics and engineers)
4. The creator economy means more people need to think professionally and independently

The thing that keeps hitting me: these ideas are 80 years old. Bush was thinking about this in 1945. Engelbart was building it in 1968. And we're only NOW getting tools that make it practical for everyday knowledge workers. The vision was always there — the technology just had to catch up.

There's something both inspiring and humbling about that. Inspiring because we're living in the moment these pioneers imagined. Humbling because the core problem — how do we think better with the help of tools? — is still largely unsolved. We have better tools than ever, but are we thinking better? That's the question I keep coming back to.
