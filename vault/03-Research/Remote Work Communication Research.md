# Remote Work Communication Research

Stats, findings, and notes for the [[Async Communication - Outline|ClearPath async communication article]].

## Key Statistics

### Meeting Overload
- Average knowledge worker attends 15.5 meetings per week (Microsoft Work Trend Index 2025)
- 67% of remote workers cite "too many meetings" as their #1 productivity killer (Buffer State of Remote Work 2025)
- Employees spend an average of 31 hours per month in unproductive meetings (Atlassian research)
- The average meeting length has increased from 43 min to 52 min since 2020 (Reclaim.ai data)

### The Async Advantage
- Teams that adopt async-first communication report 29% fewer meetings (GitLab internal data)
- Written communication leads to more equitable participation — quieter team members contribute more when they can write instead of speak (Harvard Business Review study, 2024)
- 78% of remote workers prefer async communication for routine updates and status checks (Buffer)

### Work Patterns
- The "triple peak day" — Microsoft found that many remote workers now have three productivity peaks: morning, afternoon, and evening (~9-10pm). Async communication supports these natural rhythms.
- Deep work requires ~23 minutes to regain focus after an interruption (UC Irvine study, often cited). Meetings create guaranteed interruptions.
- Teams with "no meeting days" report 73% higher productivity on those days (MIT Sloan study)

## Qualitative Findings

### Why Async Works
- Forces clearer thinking — you have to organize your thoughts to write them down
- Creates documentation automatically — no more "what did we decide in that meeting?"
- Respects time zones and different schedules
- Reduces "meeting about a meeting" syndrome
- Allows people to respond when they're in the right headspace, not when the calendar dictates

### Why People Resist Async
- Fear of missing context or nuance
- "It's faster to just hop on a call" (is it though?)
- Managers fear losing visibility/control
- Some conversations genuinely are better synchronous
- Writing is harder than talking for some people
- Cultural inertia — "this is how we've always done it"

### What GitLab Does (Case Study)
- Everything documented in their public handbook
- Meetings are optional; agendas and notes are required if you hold one
- Default to issues and merge requests for technical decisions
- "Handbook-first" approach: if it's not in the handbook, it doesn't exist as policy
- They're one of the largest fully-remote companies (~2,000 employees)

## Useful Frameworks
- **The Communication Spectrum:** Not all communication is equal. Match the medium to the message.
  - Low urgency + low complexity → async text (chat, email)
  - Low urgency + high complexity → async document (shared doc, detailed write-up)
  - High urgency + low complexity → quick sync (phone call, quick video)
  - High urgency + high complexity → scheduled meeting with agenda
- **The 24-hour rule:** If a message doesn't need a response within 24 hours, it should definitely be async
