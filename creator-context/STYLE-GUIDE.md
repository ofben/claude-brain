# Alex Rivera — Writing Style Guide

A reference for how I write, how I want to write, and the principles I try to hold across all my work. I adjust tone and format by context, but the underlying instincts stay the same.

---

## Voice

**The core:** Conversational but substantive. Like explaining something genuinely interesting to a smart friend over coffee. I never talk down, and I never show off.

I want readers to feel like they're thinking alongside me, not being lectured at. The best compliment I get is "I never thought about it that way" — not "wow, you're so smart."

### Voice Principles

- **Clarity over cleverness.** If a sentence sounds impressive but takes two reads to parse, rewrite it.
- **Specificity over abstraction.** "She checks her phone 47 times before lunch" beats "people are addicted to their phones."
- **Honesty over authority.** Say what I know, what I don't, and what I'm still figuring out. Readers trust writers who think in public.
- **Warmth over distance.** Even in professional B2B writing, there's a human on the other end. Write to them.

---

## Tone Ranges

I write for different audiences and the tone adjusts accordingly. Here's how I think about the spectrum:

### Warm / Personal — Newsletter, Bloom Studio
- First person is natural here. "I" shows up often.
- Sharing process, uncertainty, and lessons learned is a feature, not a bug.
- Emotional resonance matters. Readers should feel seen, not just informed.
- Light humor is welcome. Self-deprecation works better than snark.
- Example: "I spent three hours reorganizing my notes app last Tuesday instead of writing. Then I wrote about reorganizing my notes app. Productivity is a strange loop."

### Authoritative / Researched — Horizon Magazine
- Third person or reported style. "I" appears rarely and only for framing.
- Every claim is supported by a source, interview, or data point.
- Analytical but not cold. The reader should feel the writer cares about the subject, not just the byline.
- Longer sentences are acceptable here when the complexity demands it, but never convoluted.
- Example: "When cognitive scientist Barbara Tversky describes spatial thinking as 'the foundation of thought,' she's pointing at something most productivity tools miss entirely."

### Professional / Actionable — ClearPath
- Second person throughout. "You" and "your team."
- Every section earns its place by being useful. No filler, no tangents.
- Data and statistics add credibility. Cite them.
- End with clear takeaways. The reader should be able to do something with what they just read.
- Conversational enough to not feel like a white paper, structured enough to skim.
- Example: "If your team spends more than 30% of the workweek in meetings, async communication isn't a nice-to-have — it's a survival strategy."

## Formatting

### Structure

- **Headers for scanability.** Every major section gets an H2. Subsections get H3 if the piece is long enough. No one reads 2,000 words straight through on a screen.
- **Short paragraphs.** 2-4 sentences max. One idea per paragraph. White space is a feature.
- **Bullet points for lists.** If there are more than 3 parallel items, bullet them. Don't bury a list in prose.
- **Pull quotes or callouts for emphasis.** Use blockquotes for key insights that deserve a pause. Not every piece needs them, but they work well in longer features.
- **Bold for key phrases**, not whole sentences. Bold is a highlighter, not a megaphone.

### General Rules

- One space after periods (not two).
- Em dashes for asides — like this — not parentheses (unless the aside is truly parenthetical and minor).
- Oxford comma, always.
- Numbers under 10 are spelled out. 10 and above are numerals. Exceptions: data points and statistics always use numerals for impact.
- Contractions are fine and encouraged. "Don't" over "do not" unless the formality demands it (rare).

## Sentence-Level Craft

### Length and Rhythm

- **Mix it up.** Alternate between short punchy sentences and longer, flowing ones. Monotone rhythm puts readers to sleep regardless of what the words say.
- **Hard ceiling: 25 words per sentence.** If a sentence exceeds this, split it or rewrite it. There are almost no exceptions.
- **Short sentences for impact.** After a complex idea, land it with something brief. "That changes everything." or "It didn't work."
- **Read it aloud.** If I stumble over a sentence, my readers will too.

### Word Choice

**Words I reach for (because they do real work):**
- "Friction" — describes resistance in a system without being dramatic
- "Intentional" — implies deliberate choice, the opposite of default behavior
- "Signal vs. noise" — frames information quality without jargon
- "Workflow" — specific and practical, not buzzwordy when used precisely
- "Scaffold" — implies structure that supports growth, then can be removed

**Words I avoid (because they're empty calories):**
- "Leverage" — corporate speak. Say "use" or be more specific about what's happening.
- "Synergy" — has meant nothing since 2005.
- "Paradigm" — unless quoting Thomas Kuhn directly.
- "Disrupt" — overused to the point of self-parody.
- "Hack" — as in "life hack" or "productivity hack." It implies shortcuts and tricks. I write about systems and practice.
- "Utilize" — "use" exists. Use it.
- "Robust" — almost always filler. What does it actually mean in this sentence?
- "In today's fast-paced world" — the opening line of a thousand boring articles. Never.

## Openings

The first two sentences decide whether someone keeps reading. I take them seriously.

### What Works

- **Start with a specific moment.** Drop the reader into a scene. "At 2 a.m. on a Tuesday, I found the note that solved a problem I'd been stuck on for three weeks."
- **Start with a question.** Not rhetorical — genuine. "What happens to your best ideas after you write them down?"
- **Start with a counterintuitive observation.** Challenge an assumption. "The most productive writers I know don't use productivity systems."
- **Start with a surprising fact or statistic.** "The average knowledge worker spends 9.3 hours per week just searching for information they already have."

### What Doesn't

- "In today's fast-paced world..." (banned)
- "Have you ever wondered..." (lazy)
- "According to Webster's dictionary..." (we're not in a high school speech)
- Throat-clearing introductions that take 200 words to get to the point (just start at the point)
- Overly broad statements about technology or society without grounding them in something specific

## Structure Preference

My default structure for most pieces, adjusted by context:

### Problem - Exploration - Insight - Takeaway

1. **Problem:** Name the tension, the gap, or the question. Why should the reader care? Make it specific and relatable.
2. **Exploration:** Dig into it. Bring in research, interviews, examples, counterarguments. This is where the substance lives. Don't rush this section.
3. **Insight:** The turn. What did I find, realize, or connect that reframes the problem? This is the part readers remember and share.
4. **Takeaway:** What can the reader do with this? Not always a to-do list — sometimes the takeaway is a new way of seeing. But it should be concrete enough to be useful.

This isn't rigid. Some pieces skip straight from problem to takeaway. Some are structured as narratives. But when in doubt, this is the framework I reach for.

## Context-Specific Notes

### Newsletter (The Synthesis)

- More personal than any client work. This is where my voice is most fully itself.
- Uses "I" freely. Shares process, mistakes, half-formed ideas.
- The signature move: connect 2-3 seemingly unrelated ideas into a single theme. A concept from architecture, a finding from cognitive science, and something that happened to me last week — woven together.
- Tone is like a thoughtful email to a friend who's into the same stuff.
- Always includes a "Signal" section at the end: 3-5 links with brief commentary. Not a link dump — each one should connect to the issue's theme or be genuinely useful.
- Length: 800-1,200 words for the main essay. Keep it focused. Better to explore one idea well than three ideas superficially.

### Blog Posts (Bloom, ClearPath)

- Second person: "you" and "your."
- Actionable above all. Every section should earn its place by being useful.
- Include a TL;DR at the top for skimmers. 2-3 sentences max.
- Scannable: headers, bullets, bold key phrases. Assume the reader will skim first and decide whether to read deeply.
- End with a clear next step, not a vague "think about this."

### Features (Horizon Magazine)

- This is where I do my best writing. Give it the time and craft it deserves.
- Research depth matters. At least 3 primary sources (interviews, studies, data).
- Let the reporting breathe. Don't rush through quotes and examples to get to my opinion. The sources *are* the story.
- Scene-setting is valuable. Put the reader somewhere. "When I call Martinez on a Thursday afternoon, she's just closed 14 browser tabs..."
- Can be up to 3,500 words. Use the space, but don't waste it.

## Research Approach

- **Primary sources over hot takes.** An interview with someone doing the thing is worth more than ten opinion pieces about the thing.
- **Every substantial claim gets support.** A study, an expert quote, a concrete example. "Many people feel overwhelmed by technology" is lazy. "A 2025 APA survey found that 64% of workers describe their relationship with digital tools as 'a constant negotiation'" does actual work.
- **Include at least one expert quote or study in every published piece.** Even shorter blog posts. It signals rigor and gives the reader confidence.
- **Read the actual study, not just the headline.** Misrepresenting research is worse than not citing it.
- **Diverse sources.** Talk to people who disagree with each other. The best pieces hold tension between perspectives rather than flattening it into one take.
- **Save everything.** Even if a quote or data point doesn't make it into this piece, it might be useful in 6 months. (In theory. In practice, my research archive is a mess. Working on it.)

## Revision Checklist

Before submitting any piece, I run through this:

- [ ] Does the opening earn the next sentence?
- [ ] Is every section doing work? Cut anything that exists because "it should be there."
- [ ] Have I supported claims with evidence, not just assertion?
- [ ] Does the structure flow, or does the reader have to make leaps?
- [ ] Are sentences under 25 words? (Check the long ones especially.)
- [ ] Did I use any banned words? Search for them.
- [ ] Read it aloud. Fix anything that trips up the tongue.
- [ ] Is the ending an ending, or did I just stop writing?
- [ ] Would I want to read this? Honestly?
