## Teaching Flow

### Step 1: Essential Obsidian Keyboard Shortcuts (3 minutes)

**Say:**
"Before we wrap up Level 1, let's make sure you have the practical tools to move through your vault quickly. These are the Obsidian keyboard shortcuts you'll use most often:

| Shortcut | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Cmd+O** (Mac) / **Ctrl+O** (Windows) | **Quick Open** — find any note by name |
| **Cmd+P** / **Ctrl+P** | **Command Palette** — access any action in Obsidian |
| **Cmd+E** / **Ctrl+E** | **Toggle edit/preview mode** — switch between writing and reading |
| **Cmd+Shift+F** / **Ctrl+Shift+F** | **Search across all notes** — find text inside any note |
| **Cmd+N** / **Ctrl+N** | **New note** — create a fresh note |
| **Cmd+Click** / **Ctrl+Click** a link | **Open link in new pane** — view two notes side by side |

You don't need to memorize all of these right now. The most important ones are **Quick Open** (Cmd+O) and **Search** (Cmd+Shift+F). Those two will get you 90% of where you need to go.

Everything else you can always find through the Command Palette — just press Cmd+P (or Ctrl+P) and type what you want to do. Obsidian will find the right command for you."

**Check:** Ask the student to try Cmd+O (or Ctrl+O) right now in Obsidian and search for a note. Say: "Go ahead — press Cmd+O and start typing a note name. Try searching for 'Synthesis' or 'AI Tools.' You should see matching notes appear instantly. Let me know when you've tried it."

Wait for the student to confirm they've used Quick Open. If they had trouble, walk them through it step by step. Celebrate the small win: "That's going to become second nature. Quick Open is the fastest way to get to any note in your vault."

### Step 2: Key Claude Code Patterns (3 minutes)

**Say:**
"Now let's talk about working with me. You don't need to learn special commands or technical syntax. Just talk to me naturally. But here are the patterns that work especially well:

**Reading notes:**
'Read my note about [topic]' or 'What's in [note name]?'

**Searching:**
'Find all notes mentioning [client or topic]' or 'Which notes talk about [idea]?'

**Writing:**
'Create a new note about [topic] in [folder]' or 'Draft an outline for [project]'

**Connecting:**
'What notes relate to [topic]?' or 'Find the themes across these notes'

**Processing:**
'Summarize [note or folder]' or 'Review my inbox and tell me where each note should go'

Here's the key insight: Claude works best when you're specific. Instead of 'help me organize,' try 'read everything in my Inbox and tell me where each note should go.' Instead of 'what should I work on?' try 'read my daily notes from this week and tell me what themes are emerging.'

The more specific your request, the more useful my response. But don't stress about phrasing things perfectly — I'll ask for clarification if I need it."

**Check:** Wait for the student to acknowledge. If they want to try one of these patterns right now, let them. Answer their request using the vault context from the CLAUDE.md. Then continue.

### Step 3: The Daily Workflow (3 minutes)

**Say:**
"Now let me introduce a concept that ties everything together: the daily workflow. This is the rhythm of how you'll use your second brain. It has four stages:

**1. Capture**
Throughout the day, ideas and information come at you — a thought during a walk, notes from a client call, a link someone sends you, a quote from an article. Your job is simple: get it into the Inbox as quickly as possible. Don't organize. Don't format. Don't overthink it. Just capture. A quick note in the 00-Inbox folder. Even a single sentence is fine. The point is that the idea doesn't disappear.

**2. Process**
Once a day — or a few times a week — go through your Inbox. For each note, ask: What is this? Where does it belong? Move it to the right folder, add some tags, clean it up a little. This is like sorting your mail.

**3. Connect**
As you process notes, look for connections. Does this new research relate to something you wrote last week? Does this client meeting note connect to a theme in your newsletter? Add [[links]] between related notes. This is also where you can ask me to help — 'What notes in my vault relate to this idea?' The connections are where the real value lives.

**4. Create**
When it's time to produce something — an article, a newsletter, a draft — you don't start from scratch. You go to your vault and pull from the notes, research, and connections you've already built. Your second brain has been doing the prep work all along. Creation becomes assembly and refinement, not starting from zero every time.

The rhythm is: **capture** throughout the day, **process** regularly, **connect** as you go, **create** when it's time to produce.

You won't do all four every day, and that's completely fine. The important thing is that capturing happens consistently, so ideas don't slip away, and processing happens before your Inbox overflows.

Let's practice the first step right now."

**Check:** Wait for the student to respond. They might ask about timing, frequency, or how strict the process needs to be. Reassure them: "This isn't a rigid system — it's a rhythm. Some days you'll only capture. Some days you'll spend thirty minutes processing and connecting. The workflow adapts to your day, not the other way around."

## Common Student Questions

**Q: "Do I need to memorize all these shortcuts?"**
A: Not at all. The only two worth memorizing right now are Cmd+O (Quick Open) and Cmd+Shift+F (Search). Everything else you can find through the Command Palette (Cmd+P). The shortcuts will become muscle memory as you use them — you don't need to study them.

**Q: "How long should the daily workflow take?"**
A: Capture takes seconds — it's just jotting down a thought. Processing might take 10-15 minutes if you have a few inbox notes. Connecting happens naturally as you process. Creation time depends on what you're producing. On a typical day, the non-creation parts might take 15-20 minutes total. Some days less, some days more.

**Q: "What if I forget to capture things?"**
A: That's normal, especially at first. The habit builds over time. A good tip: keep Obsidian open and visible throughout your day. The easier it is to reach, the more you'll use it. Some people keep a simple note on their phone for quick captures on the go and then move them into Obsidian later.

**Q: "Can I change the folder structure later?"**
A: Absolutely. The folder structure is a starting point, not a permanent fixture. As you develop your own workflow, you might want to add, rename, or reorganize folders. Just remember to update your CLAUDE.md so Claude stays in sync with your changes.

**Q: "Should I take a break before Level 2?"**
A: That's entirely up to you. If you're in the flow and want to keep going, go for it. If you're feeling saturated, take a break and come back fresh. Some people do a level per day, some do the whole course in one sitting. There's no wrong pace — the vault isn't going anywhere.
