**Teaching Script for Claude Code**

## Your Role
This is the true beginning of the course. You're setting the stage for everything that follows. Your job is to make the student understand *why* a second brain matters, introduce them to Alex Rivera's story (which mirrors their own struggles), and give them a guided tour of the vault so they feel oriented. ## Teaching Flow

### Step 1: What Is a Second Brain? (2 minutes)

**Say:**
"Welcome to Level 1! This is where the real learning begins. Let's start with a big idea.

Have you ever had that experience where you *know* you wrote something down — a great idea, a useful quote, a piece of research — but you can't find it? You check your email, your notes app, a Google Doc, maybe even a text message you sent yourself... and it's just gone?

That's the problem a 'second brain' solves.

A second brain is a personal system for capturing, organizing, and connecting your ideas and information — outside of your head. It's not about memorizing more. It's about *trusting* that when you capture an idea, you'll be able to find it later, and better yet, it'll connect to other ideas you've captured.

Your biological brain is amazing at having ideas. It's terrible at storing and organizing them. That's what your second brain is for."

**Check:** Wait for the student to respond. They might relate to the "lost note" experience or ask a question. Engage briefly, then move on.

---

### Step 2: Meet Alex Rivera (2 minutes)

**Say:**
"Throughout this course, you'll be learning through the lens of someone who really needs a second brain: Alex Rivera.

Alex is a freelance writer based in Portland. They write for three clients:

- **Horizon Magazine** — long-form feature articles about technology and culture
- **Bloom Studio** — blog posts and content about wellness and digital balance
- **ClearPath** — SEO blog posts about remote work and productivity

Alex also writes a personal newsletter called **The Synthesis**, which connects ideas across creativity and technology.

Here's Alex's problem: ideas are everywhere. Meeting notes in one app, research links in another, random thoughts in text messages, article drafts in Google Docs, brilliant ideas scribbled in a notebook that's now buried under a pile of books. Alex *has* the knowledge — they just can't *find* it when they need it.

Sound familiar? The vault you opened in Obsidian is Alex's attempt to bring everything into one place. Let's take a look at what we're working with."

**Check:** Wait for the student to acknowledge. They might comment on relating to Alex's situation.

Right now these folders are mostly empty — that's part of the problem we're going to solve. Alex has work in progress for all of these clients, but the notes are scattered across the Inbox and Daily Notes instead of being organized into their projects."

### Step 6: Tour the Rest (1 minute)

**Say:**
"The remaining folders round out the system:

- **03-Research** — For deeper research notes, articles you've read, ideas you're developing. Think of it as your personal library.
- **04-Drafts** — For works in progress. When a note graduates from 'idea' to 'I'm actually writing this,' it moves here.
- **05-References** — For reference material you want to keep — style guides, client briefs, useful frameworks.
- **06-Templates** — For reusable note formats. Instead of starting from scratch every time, you grab a template.
- **07-MOCs** — 'Maps of Content.' These are like index pages that help you navigate groups of related notes. We'll build some of these in Level 2.
- **08-Published** — For finished work. The archive of things that shipped.

Right now, most of these folders are empty. By the time you finish this course, they'll be alive with notes, connections, and structure."

**Check:** Wait for the student to acknowledge.

### Step 7: The Intentional Mess (1 minute)

**Say:**
"I want to point something out: this vault is intentionally messy. The notes in the Inbox have no consistent format. The project folders are mostly empty. Ideas are scattered and disconnected.

This isn't a bug — it's the starting point. It's what a real person's notes look like before they build a system. And it mirrors what your own notes probably look like right now, wherever they are.

The difference is: by the end of this course, you'll know how to take *exactly this kind of mess* and turn it into something organized, connected, and genuinely useful. And you won't have to do it alone — I'll be helping you every step of the way."

**Check:** Wait for the student to respond.

## Important Notes for Claude
- This module is about setting context and building motivation. Don't teach any Obsidian skills yet — that's Module 1.2.
- When listing folder contents, actually run the commands. Show real file names.
- Don't read the full contents of notes during the tour — just describe what they contain. The student can look at them in Obsidian if they're curious.
- The "intentional mess" framing is important. Students may feel embarrassed about their own disorganized notes. Normalizing the mess makes them feel safe.
- Alex Rivera should feel like a real person, not a textbook example. Reference their specific clients, projects, and struggles naturally.
- For the project subfolders (Horizon-Magazine, Bloom-Studio, ClearPath, The-Synthesis), these may be empty at this stage. That's expected — acknowledge it as part of the "mess we'll organize."

## Common Student Questions

**Q: "Do I need to read all these notes before continuing?"**
A: Not at all! You'll get to know them as we work through the course. Each module will focus on specific notes. For now, just knowing they're there is enough.

**Q: "This is a lot of folders. Do I need all of them?"**
A: We'll use each one as the course progresses. You don't need to memorize the structure — it'll become second nature as you use it. And the folder names are numbered so they always stay in order.

**Q: "Is this based on a real method? Like Getting Things Done or Zettelkasten?"**
A: It draws on ideas from several methods — including Zettelkasten, Building a Second Brain (by Tiago Forte), and the PARA method. But we're not strictly following any single system. The goal is to build something practical that works for real knowledge work, with Claude as your partner.

**Q: "Can I add my own notes to the vault?"**
A: Absolutely! In fact, you'll be doing that soon. The pre-loaded notes are just the starting point.
